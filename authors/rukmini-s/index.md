# Rukmini S.

Rukmini S. is a data journalist and author based in Chennai. She is the founder and director of Data for India, a public platform focused on making data and information about India more accessible and useful to researchers, policymakers, and the wider public.

She began her journalism career covering Mumbai for the Times of India and later specialised in data journalism. She was the first Data Editor at The Hindu and subsequently worked as Editor for Data and Innovation at HuffPost India. Her work combines statistical analysis with field reporting and an interest in understanding what official data can and cannot reveal about people's lives.

Her work focuses particularly on demographics, gender, household economics, inequality, caste, and politics. She has also written extensively about India's statistical systems and the interpretation of social and economic data. Her reporting has appeared in publications including Mint, IndiaSpend, The Guardian, and other Indian news organisations. ([Data for India](https://www.dataforindia.com/author/rukmini/))

Rukmini is the author of *Whole Numbers and Half Truths: What Data Can and Cannot Tell Us About Modern India*, published by Westland in 2021. The book uses official statistics, surveys, field reporting, and other evidence to examine how India is changing and where commonly used numbers can give an incomplete or misleading picture of society. ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Whole_Numbers_And_Half_Truths_What_Data.html?id=u4EUEQAAQBAJ))

Her work has received several awards, including recognition at the Sigma Awards for Data Journalism and the Asian College of Journalism Awards. Her pandemic reporting and analysis of India's COVID-19 data also received recognition from health and policy communities. ([NALSAR University of Law](https://nalsar.ac.in/event/nalsarindialogue-with-rukmini-s-working-with-socio-economic-data-in-india/))

Her approach to journalism emphasises the importance of understanding the context behind statistics. Rather than treating numbers as self-explanatory, her work examines how data are collected, what they measure, what they leave out, and how they can shape public understanding of India.
